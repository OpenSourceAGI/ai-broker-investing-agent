## ETF and portfolio foundation

**1. *The Little Book of Common Sense Investing* — John C. Bogle.**  
Bogle’s central proposition is that an investor should capture the return produced by the whole market rather than try to identify the few managers or stocks that will outperform it. Because all investors collectively own the market before costs, active investors collectively earn the market return before expenses—but pay management fees, trading costs, taxes, and turnover afterward. A broad index fund therefore provides an investor’s “fair share” of market returns at minimal cost. The book emphasizes that small annual costs compound into large losses over decades, while broad diversification eliminates company-specific and manager-selection risk. Its behavioral instruction is equally important: buy a low-cost total-market fund or similarly broad index vehicle, hold it through booms and crashes, and avoid turning an investment plan into a trading habit. [investmentnews](https://www.investmentnews.com/guides/why-the-little-book-of-common-sense-investing-still-matters-in-todays-market/264782)

**2. *The Bogleheads’ Guide to Investing* — Taylor Larimore, Mel Lindauer, Michael LeBoeuf.**  
This book converts Bogle’s broad philosophy into a practical household system. Its key claim is that successful investing is less about finding brilliant securities than establishing simple, repeatable rules around saving, asset allocation, account selection, diversification, taxes, costs, and rebalancing. It generally favors a small collection of broad, low-cost stock and bond index funds, held in proportions appropriate to the investor’s time horizon, spending needs, and willingness to tolerate declines. The book also highlights tax-advantaged accounts, disciplined rebalancing instead of market timing, and the importance of insulating decisions from fear, greed, headlines, and recent performance. The practical takeaway is to make a plan you can follow during a severe bear market—not merely one that looks optimal in a spreadsheet.

**3. *A Random Walk Down Wall Street* — Burton G. Malkiel.**  
Malkiel explains why consistently identifying mispriced securities is exceptionally difficult: widely available information is rapidly incorporated into prices by competing investors, analysts, and institutions. He surveys fundamental analysis, technical analysis, bubbles, speculative manias, and the efficient-market hypothesis, while acknowledging that markets can be irrational and volatile in the short run. The important distinction is that markets need not be perfectly efficient for outperformance to be hard: even genuine pricing mistakes may be too uncertain, too brief, or too expensive to exploit after costs. The book therefore argues for diversification, regular investing, and broad index funds for most people, while warning against performance chasing and confident forecasts. Its core lesson is that uncertainty should be managed with portfolio construction, not ignored because a story about a company sounds persuasive.

**4. *The Four Pillars of Investing* — William J. Bernstein.**  
Bernstein organizes investing around four pillars: investment theory, financial history, investment psychology, and the investment business. The theory section explains expected return, diversification, risk, and the relationship between stocks and bonds. The history section shows that crashes, bubbles, and long disappointing stretches are normal features of markets rather than unprecedented failures of one’s plan. The psychology section focuses on the ways investors systematically undermine themselves, especially by buying what has recently risen and selling after declines. Finally, the business section explains why many financial products and intermediaries are structured to extract fees rather than maximize client outcomes. The key point is that a sound portfolio must survive all four dimensions: it must be economically defensible, historically realistic, behaviorally tolerable, and inexpensive to maintain.

**5. *All About Asset Allocation* — Richard A. Ferri.**  
Ferri treats asset allocation—the mix of stocks, bonds, inflation-sensitive assets, domestic and foreign holdings—as the investor’s most consequential controllable decision. Rather than presenting a single universally correct portfolio, he explains how diversification works across imperfectly correlated assets and how allocation should reflect goals, time horizon, liquidity needs, inflation risk, and capacity to endure volatility. The book gives practical attention to rebalancing rules, implementation through low-cost funds, and the tradeoff between simplicity and granularity. Its central message is that a portfolio is not a collection of exciting investments; it is a risk-management system. An investor should first decide the role each holding plays, then select low-cost instruments to fill those roles.

**6. *The Intelligent Asset Allocator* — William J. Bernstein.**  
This more quantitative book builds the case that portfolio outcomes depend on expected returns, volatility, and correlations—not on isolated predictions about individual holdings. Bernstein explains why a collection of risky assets can be safer than a concentrated bet when the assets do not move in lockstep, and why expected return should be evaluated at the portfolio level. He also emphasizes the harsh arithmetic of losses: a large drawdown requires a disproportionately large gain to recover, so avoiding excessive risk can be more important than maximizing headline return. The main lesson is to choose a diversified allocation with an expected return sufficient for your objectives and a drawdown profile you can actually live with. It is an antidote to evaluating investments one at a time without considering their contribution to total portfolio risk.

## Behavior and market judgment

**7. *The Psychology of Money* — Morgan Housel.**  
Housel argues that financial success often depends more on behavior than intelligence. People make money decisions through the lens of their own upbringing, luck, fear, incentives, and personal experiences, so apparently irrational choices can make sense within a person’s history. The book stresses savings rate, patience, endurance, and the ability to avoid financial ruin. It distinguishes being “rich,” meaning having high spending power or visible wealth, from being “wealthy,” meaning having unspent assets and optionality. Its most useful investing principle is that compounding needs time, and time requires survival: avoiding leverage, overconcentration, and decisions that can permanently remove you from the game may matter more than maximizing short-run returns.

**8. *Your Money and Your Brain* — Jason Zweig.**  
Zweig connects behavioral finance with neuroscience to explain why investors systematically make poor decisions under stress. Market gains stimulate reward-seeking, while losses trigger fear and pain responses that can push investors into buying at euphoric highs or selling during panic. The book examines loss aversion, overconfidence, herd behavior, recency bias, excessive trading, and the tendency to mistake luck for skill. Its practical message is to build friction and rules into your financial life before emotions take over: automate contributions, diversify, limit constant price monitoring, write an investment policy, and rebalance according to a plan. The goal is not to eliminate emotion but to prevent emotion from becoming an unreviewed trading signal.

**9. *The Most Important Thing* — Howard Marks.**  
Marks emphasizes “second-level thinking”: not merely asking whether a company or asset is good, but asking what the market already believes, what is embedded in the price, and what could make consensus wrong. He argues that investment risk is not synonymous with volatility; risk is primarily the possibility of permanent loss, impaired capital, or failure to meet one’s objectives. The book repeatedly returns to cycles, valuation, credit conditions, investor psychology, and asymmetry—situations where downside is limited relative to potential upside. Marks is skeptical of precise forecasting and argues that strong investors focus on recognizing their limits, controlling exposures, demanding a margin of safety, and avoiding severe mistakes. The lesson is that investing well often means losing less during bad periods, not predicting every turn correctly.

## Individual-stock analysis

**10. *Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits* — Philip A. Fisher.**  
Fisher presents a qualitative method for identifying exceptional businesses capable of compounding over long periods. His framework focuses on management quality, research and development, sales organization, profit margins, competitive position, labor relations, accounting integrity, and the company’s opportunity to reinvest capital at attractive rates. He is associated with “scuttlebutt” research: gathering evidence from customers, suppliers, competitors, former employees, and industry participants rather than relying only on financial statements. The central idea is that paying a fair price for an unusually durable, well-managed growth company can be more attractive than buying a statistically cheap but deteriorating business. The caveat is that qualitative research can easily slide into narrative bias, so the reader should pair Fisher’s ideas with disciplined valuation and skepticism.

**11. *The Intelligent Investor* — Benjamin Graham.**  
Graham’s book is the foundational case for disciplined value investing and emotional restraint. His “Mr. Market” metaphor teaches that the market is an unreliable business partner who offers to buy or sell every day at changing prices; the investor should use those quotes when they are favorable, not treat them as authoritative judgments of value. His “margin of safety” principle requires buying securities at prices sufficiently below a conservative estimate of value to allow for analytical errors, bad luck, and unexpected events. Graham distinguishes defensive investors, who should favor simple diversified, low-turnover approaches, from enterprising investors, who are willing to perform substantial analysis and maintain strict discipline. Some individual-security examples are dated, but the enduring points are that price and value differ, forecasts are fragile, and a process must protect the investor from both market volatility and self-deception.

**12. *The Little Book That Still Beats the Market* — Joel Greenblatt.**  
Greenblatt introduces a simplified quantitative framework often called the “magic formula,” which ranks companies using a measure of business quality—typically return on capital—and a measure of cheapness—typically earnings yield. The conceptual message is more valuable than blindly copying the screen: good investing should consider both what a business earns on the capital it employs and the price paid for those earnings. A wonderful business bought at any price can be a poor investment, while a cheap company with chronically weak economics may be a value trap. The book also demonstrates that even a historically successful systematic strategy can experience painful multi-year underperformance, making behavioral discipline essential. It should be read as an introduction to factor-style thinking, not evidence that a formula will reliably outperform after crowding, taxes, transaction costs, and changing market conditions.

**13. *Security Analysis* — Benjamin Graham and David Dodd.**  
This is the deep reference work for fundamental investing. Graham and Dodd separate investment from speculation by insisting that an investment should rest on thorough analysis, safety of principal, and an adequate expected return. They focus on scrutinizing balance sheets, debt obligations, asset values, earnings stability, coverage ratios, accounting quality, and the difference between recurring earning power and temporary results. Their approach is deliberately conservative because financial statements and forecasts are uncertain; the answer is not to make more precise predictions than the evidence supports, but to require a margin of safety. The book is most useful for developing research habits: normalize earnings, test downside scenarios, examine incentives and capital structure, and avoid treating a single valuation metric as a complete thesis.

**14. *Investment Valuation* — Aswath Damodaran.**  
Damodaran frames valuation as disciplined storytelling constrained by numbers. Every valuation ultimately makes claims about cash flows, growth, competitive advantage, reinvestment, risk, and the rate used to discount future cash flows. The book explains discounted cash flow analysis, relative valuation multiples, cost of capital, terminal value, option-like characteristics, and sector-specific challenges. A key insight is that valuation is not a mechanical exercise: a spreadsheet can produce a precise output from assumptions that are deeply uncertain. Analysts should therefore expose their assumptions, connect growth to reinvestment and returns on capital, use scenarios and sensitivity analysis, and distinguish value from price. The purpose is not to calculate a magically exact intrinsic value, but to develop a transparent range of plausible values and understand what must happen for an investment to work.

**15. *The Essays of Warren Buffett* — edited by Lawrence A. Cunningham.**  
This collection organizes Buffett’s shareholder letters into a coherent philosophy of owning businesses rather than trading tickers. The principal themes include rational capital allocation, durable competitive advantages, management integrity, incentive design, accounting quality, shareholder-friendly governance, and the compounding value of retained earnings when reinvested well. Buffett repeatedly emphasizes that business performance and stock performance are related over the long run but can diverge substantially in the short run; therefore, investors should assess economics rather than react to price movements. He also warns about excessive leverage, empire-building acquisitions, opaque accounting, and executive compensation that rewards activity over value creation. The lasting lesson is to judge management by how intelligently it deploys each dollar of shareholder capital, not by charisma or quarterly promises.

**16. *Against the Gods* — Peter L. Bernstein.**  
Bernstein traces the historical development of probability, insurance, statistics, portfolio theory, and modern risk management. The book’s central contribution is conceptual: uncertainty is not merely an inconvenience but a condition that can be measured, priced, pooled, and managed—though never eliminated. It explains how thinkers from Pascal and Bernoulli through Markowitz, Sharpe, and others transformed decision-making from reliance on fate into analysis of odds, expected outcomes, and tradeoffs. For investors, the key message is that risk is multidimensional: volatility, probability of loss, liquidity, leverage, correlation, and the consequences of being wrong all matter. It is especially helpful preparation for prediction markets because it develops the distinction between estimating probabilities and making a good decision under uncertainty.

## Evidence on investing

**17. “The Arithmetic of Active Management” — William F. Sharpe (1991).**  
Sharpe’s paper makes a simple but powerful identity argument. Before costs, the aggregate holdings of active investors are the market portfolio, because active investors collectively own every security not held by passive investors. Therefore, before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn the market return. Once management fees, trading costs, bid–ask spreads, research expenses, and taxes are deducted, the average active dollar must underperform the average passive dollar. This does not prove that no investor can outperform; it proves that above-average performance must be offset by below-average performance among other active investors, and that the group as a whole loses after costs. The practical implication is that anyone paying for active management should ask for unusually strong, persistent, net-of-fee evidence—not merely a compelling story or recent outperformance.

**18. “The Cross-Section of Expected Stock Returns” — Eugene Fama and Kenneth French (1992).**  
Fama and French examine why average returns differ across stocks and find that market beta alone does not explain the cross-section as well as the traditional Capital Asset Pricing Model suggested. They show that firm size and the book-to-market ratio—often interpreted as a value characteristic—were strongly related to historical average returns in their sample. Smaller companies and companies with high book-to-market ratios tended to have higher average returns than larger or “growth” companies with low book-to-market ratios. The paper became a foundation for factor investing, but its message is not that simple labels guarantee future outperformance. Factor premiums can vary for long periods, may reflect risk or behavioral effects, and can be reduced by trading costs, crowding, taxes, and data mining.

**19. “Common Risk Factors in the Returns on Stocks and Bonds” — Fama and French (1993).**  
This paper formalizes the three-factor model, which explains stock returns using exposure to the overall market, a size factor, and a value factor. Its major contribution is evaluative: an investment manager who appears to have generated “alpha” may instead have taken systematic exposure to small-cap or value stocks. That distinction matters because factor exposures can often be obtained more cheaply and transparently through diversified funds than through a high-fee active manager. The broader lesson is that performance should be decomposed. Investors should not infer skill just because a fund outperformed a benchmark; they should ask what risks and factor tilts created that return, whether the comparison benchmark is appropriate, and whether the result persists after fees.

**20. “Returns to Buying Winners and Selling Losers” — Narasimhan Jegadeesh and Sheridan Titman (1993).**  
This study documents medium-term momentum: portfolios of stocks that performed well over the prior several months tended, on average in the studied data, to continue outperforming portfolios of recent losers over subsequent months. The result challenged the idea that all publicly observable return information is instantly and fully reflected in prices. One possible interpretation is that investor underreaction, gradual information diffusion, or behavioral biases cause trends to persist temporarily. Yet momentum is not a frictionless strategy: it tends to require turnover, can incur taxes and trading costs, and is known for occasional severe reversals or “momentum crashes.” The paper’s practical value is as evidence that markets have patterns, not as a promise that a retail investor can easily capture them.

**21. “On Persistence in Mutual Fund Performance” — Mark Carhart (1997).**  
Carhart extends factor-based performance evaluation and shows that much apparent mutual-fund persistence can be explained by common factor exposures, particularly momentum, along with costs and expenses. A fund’s recent outperformance may reflect holdings that benefited from a known systematic return pattern rather than repeatable stock-picking ability. The paper also strengthens the warning against chasing top-ranked funds: the investors most likely to buy after a strong streak may arrive after the favorable period has already occurred, while fees remain certain. Its main contribution is methodological. Evaluate managers net of costs, compare them against relevant factor-adjusted benchmarks, and distinguish repeatable skill from temporary exposure, luck, or an investment style that has simply been in favor.

## Prediction markets and quant research

**22. “Prediction Markets in Theory and Practice” — Justin Wolfers and Eric Zitzewitz.**  
Wolfers and Zitzewitz explain prediction markets as venues where contracts linked to future outcomes turn dispersed information and incentives into tradable prices. If a properly designed binary contract pays \$1 if an event occurs and \$0 otherwise, a price near \$0.63 can often be interpreted as an approximate market-implied 63% chance—but only with qualifications about liquidity, fees, risk preferences, contract rules, trader composition, and market design. Their review finds that prediction-market forecasts have often been fairly accurate and can outperform many moderately sophisticated benchmarks, particularly when contracts are clear and participation is sufficiently informed. They also show that market design can elicit beliefs about outcomes beyond a simple yes/no event, including expected values, medians, uncertainty, and conditional relationships. The key point is that market prices are useful aggregated forecasts, not infallible probabilities. [aeaweb](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/0895330041371321)

**23. “Information Markets: A New Way of Making Decisions” — Robert W. Hahn and Paul C. Tetlock.**  
Hahn and Tetlock consider prediction markets as decision-support tools for governments, firms, and other institutions rather than merely as speculative products. Their central argument is that markets can aggregate fragmented knowledge from people with different information and incentives, creating a continuously updated forecast that may reveal problems or opportunities conventional hierarchies overlook. They also stress the conditions required for usefulness: clear contract definitions, adequate liquidity, appropriate participation, legal and ethical governance, resistance to manipulation, and sensible interpretation by decision-makers. A thin or poorly designed market can produce noisy prices, while a market focused on the wrong measurable outcome can encourage the wrong behavior. The practical takeaway is to treat an internal market as one input into judgment and forecasting, not as a substitute for accountability, domain expertise, or causal analysis.

**24. *The Wisdom of Crowds* — James Surowiecki.**  
Surowiecki’s thesis is that groups can outperform experts when individual judgments are diverse, sufficiently independent, decentralized, and combined through a mechanism that aggregates them well. The important mechanism is error cancellation: different people bring different information and biases, so their independent mistakes may offset one another. Prediction markets are one potential aggregation mechanism because participants reveal beliefs through prices and face incentives to be accurate. But the book’s caveat is as important as its optimism: crowds fail when members imitate one another, draw from the same flawed information, lack relevant expertise, face distorted incentives, or cannot express disagreement. Therefore, a consensus forecast is not automatically wise; its quality depends on the information structure that produced it.

**25. “Makers or Takers: The Economics of the Kalshi Prediction Market” — Constantin Bürgi, Wanying Deng, Karl Whelan (2026 working paper).**  
This working paper studies trading and forecast quality on Kalshi, a U.S. regulated prediction-market platform. Its central findings, as described in the syllabus, are that prices tend to become more accurate as contract resolution approaches and that the market exhibits a favorite–longshot bias: low-probability contracts are relatively overpriced while high-probability contracts are relatively underpriced compared with realized outcomes. The point is not that prediction markets are useless; rather, price-as-probability must be tested for calibration rather than assumed. A contract price can reflect retail demand, liquidity conditions, incentives, fees, contract design, and market-making structure in addition to informed belief. For a forecaster, this means using market prices as valuable signals while checking calibration by probability bucket—for example, whether contracts priced near 10% actually resolve true about 10% of the time. The study reinforces the broader lesson that market forecasts improve with information and time, yet remain subject to systematic bias.  

**26. “Efficient Capital Markets: A Review of Theory and Empirical Work” — Eugene F. Fama (1970).**  
Fama’s review lays out the efficient-market hypothesis and categorizes efficiency by the information reflected in prices. In its weak form, prices incorporate past price and volume information; in its semi-strong form, they incorporate all publicly available information; and in its strong form, they even reflect private information. The central implication is not that prices are always correct or that bubbles cannot occur, but that obtaining abnormal risk-adjusted returns using broadly available information is difficult because many market participants are already competing to exploit it. The paper provides the intellectual baseline for evaluating any claimed trading edge: specify the information used, define the benchmark, adjust for risk, and measure returns after all implementation costs. It remains foundational precisely because later anomalies, factors, and behavioral findings are best understood as refinements and challenges to this benchmark rather than simple refutations of the idea that competition makes easy profits scarce.

**27. “The Limits of Arbitrage” — Andrei Shleifer and Robert Vishny (1997).**  
Shleifer and Vishny explain why rational, sophisticated investors may fail to eliminate obvious-looking mispricings. In textbook models, arbitrage is nearly riskless and requires little capital; in reality, it is risky, capital-intensive, specialized, and often performed by managers investing other people’s money. If a mispricing widens before correcting, the arbitrageur may face losses, investor withdrawals, margin pressure, or forced liquidation—even when the original thesis is ultimately right. This is particularly dangerous in extreme environments, when prices may be furthest from fundamental value but arbitrageurs are most constrained. The paper helps explain why bubbles, anomalies, and price dislocations can persist without proving that they are easy to exploit. Its essential warning for quant and prediction-market traders is that being correct about long-run value is insufficient if you cannot finance, survive, and maintain the position until convergence occurs.
